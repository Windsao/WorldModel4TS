\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We follow the zero-shot long-sequence forecasting protocol of \citet{visionts} exactly:
Time-Series-Library splits \citep{tslib} (ETT 12/4/4 months, others 70/10/20), a
\texttt{StandardScaler} fitted on the training portion, all channels, stride-1 test origins, and
horizons $H \in \{96,192,336,720\}$, with MSE and MAE computed on standardised values. Context
lengths and periods per dataset follow \citet{visionts}: ETTh1 2880/24, ETTh2 1728/24, ETTm1
2304/96, ETTm2 4032/96, electricity 2880/24, weather 4032/144. As a check on the harness we ran
their released checkpoint through our pipeline and reproduced their numbers to three decimals
(ETTh1 at $H{=}96$: $0.3527/0.3833$ against the published $0.353/0.383$). Baseline numbers are
quoted from \citet{visiontspp} Table 4 and from \citet{visionts}.

Ablations use the five datasets that fit a single evaluation budget (ETT$\times$4 and weather) at
three horizons; the full six-dataset, four-horizon grid is reserved for the main table and for
seed replication.

Table~\ref{tab:perhorizon} breaks the aggregate down, and the head-to-head in
Appendix~\ref{app:perhorizon} gives us 18 of VisionTS's 24 cells. The six losses are all
mid-to-long horizons on hourly ETT, and they share a cause with the ETTh1/ETTh2 column losses above:
at $H{=}720$ with $P{=}24$, covering the horizon requires either eight periods per frame or several
rollout passes, and both cost resolution. The wins are large and systematic elsewhere: every
electricity cell improves by $18$--$28\%$ over VisionTS, and weather at $H{=}96$ by $37\%$.

\label{sec:ablation}

Removing the value-space term and training the same backbone on the same data with the native pixel
objective costs $5\%$ at $H{=}96$, $5\%$ at $H{=}336$ and $8\%$ at $H{=}720$
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The term accounts for the entire gain of the recipe, and its value grows
with horizon, which is what one expects if the mechanism is the alignment between what is optimised
and what is scored: the longer the horizon, the more of the prediction is generated rather than
copied. Prior anchoring is inert at 32 frames.

The budget rows matter as much as the ablation rows. Under the aligned objective, error decreases
monotonically from 5k to 60k steps. Under the pixel objective it increases monotonically over the
same range, which is the subject of the next subsection. Two further attempts to buy accuracy with
scale did not work and we report them for completeness: raising the batch size to 128 or 256 is
equal or worse at every horizon, and quadrupling the in-domain corpus (adding buildings\_900k,
LargeST, Q-Traffic and Azure) ties the corpus we use.

Table~\ref{tab:diagnosis} and Figure~\ref{fig:budget}a are the result that produced the method. Continuing VideoMAE's own
objective on the rendered corpus improves its own held-out loss by $15\%$ while forecast error rises
by $12\%$. Per cell, the 176k model is $5$--$60\%$ worse than the 20k model, with the damage
concentrated at long horizons; ETTh2 at $H{=}720$ moves from $0.429$ to $0.617$.

We checked the obvious alternative explanations. The training loss is flat across the range
($0.0092 \to 0.0089$), so this is not pixel-space overfitting. Checkpoint key sets, the transformers
version, the attention configuration and loader warnings were all ruled out by re-evaluating the
same checkpoints under matched software. What does change monotonically is the weight norm: all
layers shrink by roughly $30\%$ over the $9\times$ longer schedule under AdamW's decoupled decay, so
the backbone drifts steadily away from the initialisation that made it useful while its own
objective keeps improving. The aligned objective removes the symptom, and prior anchoring, which
attacks the drift directly, helps only in the regime where the window is short
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{What the backbone contributes}
\label{sec:backbone}

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Backbone study. Identical recipe, corpus, 5k-step budget, 32-frame clips and inference

\begin{center}

\begin{tabular}{llcccc}

\midrule

V-JEPA~2.1-B & video, explicit prediction & 87M\,+\,23M & 0.252 & \textbf{0.335} & \textbf{0.382} \\
\midrule
V-JEPA~2-L & video, explicit prediction & 300M & 0.245 & 0.329 & 0.379 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Table~\ref{tab:backbone} holds everything fixed except the weights the model starts from. At matched
size, both video backbones beat the image backbone by $3$--$5\%$ at every horizon, and they are
indistinguishable from each other: $0.250$ for a reconstructive video model against $0.252$ for a
predictive one at $H{=}96$, with the order reversing at longer horizons. The advantage of V-JEPA~2-L
($0.245$) over V-JEPA~2.1-B is capacity, not objective, and we do not read it as evidence that
predictive world models beat reconstructive ones.

The random-init control is the informative row. The same ViT-L architecture, same data, same steps,
same everything, reaches $0.280$ at $H{=}96$ --- $12.5\%$ worse than its pretrained twin, and worse
than an image backbone $3.5\times$ smaller. Per dataset at $H{=}96$ it gives
$0.401/0.282/0.369/0.182/0.166$ against $0.357/0.270/0.294/0.162/0.141$ pretrained; the gap is
$10.8\%$ at $H{=}336$ and $9.3\%$ at $H{=}720$. Capacity does not explain the transfer, and neither
does the corpus, which all arms share. What is left is the prior over dynamics.

Three caveats on the V-JEPA~2.1-B arm: it is distilled from a ViT-G teacher, its predictor output
head was re-initialised because the pretrained head maps into the 1664-dimensional teacher space
while our targets are 768-dimensional ViT-B EMA features, and it was pretrained at 384px while we
render at 224.

\subsection{Seed variance and efficiency}
\label{sec:seeds}

Two seeds of the 20k model agree to within $0.002$ on the five-dataset means
($0.245/0.330/0.379$ and $0.243/0.330/0.378$), and on the full six-dataset grid they agree on five
of six datasets, with ETTm1 the only visible spread ($0.351$ against $0.360$). Averaging the two
seeds in \emph{prediction} space is worth a further $0.6\%$ and is never worse on any cell, giving
$0.286$ against the single-seed $0.288$ at 20k steps. We leave it out of Table~\ref{tab:main}, since
it doubles the training cost for a gain smaller than the step from 20k to 60k.

this as a compute claim; the models differ in size and our 32-frame clips are about $8\times$ more
expensive at inference than 16-frame clips.
