%----------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The experiments answer four questions in order: the accuracy the framework reaches under a small
time-series pretraining budget (\S\ref{sec:main}--\S\ref{sec:budget}); what the video
initialisation contributes and where that stops (\S\ref{sec:backbone}); what the transfer design
contributes (\S\ref{sec:design}); and why the naive alternative fails (\S\ref{sec:diagnosis}).

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Protocol.} We follow the zero-shot long-sequence protocol of \citet{visionts}:
Time-Series-Library splits \citep{tslib} (ETT 12/4/4 months, others 70/10/20), a
\texttt{StandardScaler} fitted on the target dataset's training portion, all channels, stride-1 test
origins, $H\in\{96,192,336,720\}$, MSE/MAE on standardised values, and their per-dataset context
lengths and periods (listed in Appendix~\ref{app:impl}). Running their released checkpoint through our harness
reproduces their published numbers to three decimals (ETTh1 at $H{=}96$: $0.3527/0.3833$ against
$0.353/0.383$). One checkpoint and one inference rule serve all 24 cells, with no parameter
updated on any evaluation dataset.

\paragraph{Ablations and baselines.} Ablations and initialisation studies use five datasets
(ETT$\times$4 and weather) at three horizons; the six-dataset four-horizon grid is reserved for the
main table. Five- and six-dataset means are never mixed, and every table says which it reports.
Baselines are quoted from \citet{visiontspp} and \citet{visionts}; we did not retrain them.

\subsection{Lowest mean MSE of the models compared, behind on mean MAE}
\label{sec:main}

\input{figures/main_table}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports four-horizon averages. Our model has the lowest mean MSE of the models
compared, $0.285$ against $0.289$ for VisionTS++ large and $0.291$ for its base model, and takes the
most first places across the twelve dataset columns. The margin comes from electricity ($0.163$ against
$0.181$ for VisionTS++ large, a $10\%$ reduction) and weather ($0.215$ against $0.222$ for
VisionTS++ base and $0.226$ for large); ETTh1 goes to VisionTS
($0.390$ against $0.404$) and ETTh2 to VisionTS++ large ($0.327$ against $0.335$), both hourly sets
at long horizons, where our 32-frame window has to trade phase resolution for coverage.

The metric picture is mixed and we state it plainly: mean MAE is $0.328$ against VisionTS++ large's
$0.326$, so on that metric we are behind, and per cell against the same-size VisionTS++ base we take
17 of 24 on MSE but only 11 on MAE (Appendix~\ref{app:perhorizon}). The claim this table supports is
that the framework reaches the leading band on MSE, not that it dominates it.

At 20k updates instead of 60k the same recipe reaches $0.288/0.331$, still first on mean MSE here at
a third of the training.


Averaged over the six datasets, error grows from $0.223/0.281$ at $H{=}96$ to $0.349/0.377$ at
$H{=}720$; Appendix~\ref{app:perhorizon} gives every cell against both baselines that publish
per-horizon numbers.

\subsection{18.9 GPU-hours on 0.3\% of the archive}
\label{sec:budget}

\input{figures/budget_table}

Table~\ref{tab:budget} states the resources on both sides in the units each paper reports. The main
model is 60k optimizer updates at an effective batch of 32 over a 654.2M-observation corpus, and it
took 18.9 hours on a single A40. Against VisionTS++'s reported 100k steps at batch 512 over the
231B-observation LOTSA archive, that is $\approx$1/27 of the sample presentations and $\approx$0.3\%
of the corpus.

We keep these quantities separate rather than collapsing them into one efficiency number, because
they are not interchangeable: the models differ in size, a sample contains a different number of
time points and visual tokens under the two renderings, and the baseline's hardware and wall-clock
are not reported. For the same reason we make no FLOP or speed-up claim. The accounting also
excludes the upstream video pretraining we inherit, exactly as the image-based systems inherit
ImageNet pretraining; what it measures is the cost of the time-series stage, which is the stage a
practitioner would have to pay for again.

\subsection{What the video initialisation is worth, and at which budget}
\label{sec:backbone}

\input{figures/backbone_table}

Table~\ref{tab:backbone} answers this with two controls, split into panels because only within a
panel is the initialisation the sole difference. In the upper panel --- same rendering, objective,
corpus, schedule and inference rule, ViT-B either way --- the video initialisation leads the ImageNet
one by $3$--$5\%$ at every horizon after 5k updates. The lower panel adds the random-init control, which is
the cleanest statement about pretraining as such: the same ViT-L architecture under the same trainer,
data and schedule reaches $0.280$ at $H{=}96$ with no pretrained weights against $0.245$ with them,
a $14\%$ gap from the weights alone. That randomly initialised ViT-L is also worse than the
pretrained ViT-B a third its size in the upper panel, which is suggestive about capacity, but that
particular comparison crosses the two trainers and we do not treat it as controlled.

Two boundaries belong with this result, and the first is consequential. The advantage is
budget-dependent: at 20k updates the image initialisation has caught up and is slightly ahead at the
longest horizon, so what video pretraining supplies is adaptation efficiency rather than a higher
ceiling. The reported 60k system sits past that crossover and we did not train a 60k image-initialised
counterpart, so this study does not establish that the headline number requires the video
initialisation --- it establishes what the video prior is worth at the budgets we ran the comparison
at. And the panels are not comparable to
each other --- the lower arms use a different trainer (latent prediction with an added predictor, an
EMA target encoder, no tube-masked batches) on a larger backbone --- so nothing here shows a
predictive video objective beating a reconstructive one; at ViT-B the two are within $0.002$. The
image-initialised arm is an initialisation control, not a reimplementation of VisionTS++.

\subsection{Value-space supervision accounts for the measured gain over pixel-only training}
\label{sec:design}

\input{figures/ablation_table}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} varies the supervision and the budget with everything else fixed. Replacing
the value-space term with pixel-only supervision --- same masking, rendering, corpus, schedule and
inference rule --- costs $5\%$ at $H{=}96$, $5\%$ at $H{=}336$ and $8\%$ at $H{=}720$. Under this
matched comparison the read-out term accounts for the measured gain of the recipe, and its value
grows with the horizon, which is where more of the output is generated rather than carried over from
the visible context. Prior anchoring \citep{l2sp} is inert at the main model's 32-frame clip length
and matters only at 16 frames (Appendix~\ref{app:16f}); we report it as an inherited regulariser
that this configuration does not need.

Under this objective the budget curve is monotonically improving: $0.250 \to 0.245 \to 0.242$ at
$H{=}96$ for 5k, 20k and 60k updates. Scaling the other two knobs did not help --- a larger effective
batch is equal or worse at every horizon and a $4\times$ larger in-domain corpus ties ours ---
Appendix~\ref{app:failed}.

\subsection{The native objective and forecasting accuracy move in opposite directions}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

\input{figures/budget_curve}

Figure~\ref{fig:budget}a is the observation that motivated the design. Continuing VideoMAE's native
masked-pixel objective on the rendered corpus lowers its held-out pixel loss monotonically over the
four checkpoints we evaluated ($0.0366 \to 0.0312$) while forecast error rises monotonically over the
three evaluated downstream ($0.339 \to 0.381$). Per cell the 176k model is $5$--$60\%$ worse than the
20k one, worst at long horizons (ETTh2 at $H{=}720$: $0.429 \to 0.617$). The 60k native-objective
checkpoint was not evaluated downstream, so that point is missing rather than interpolated, and this
is a trend over the checkpoints we measured, not a scaling law.

Alternative explanations were excluded by re-evaluating under matched software, and the training loss
is flat, so this is not pixel-space overfitting (Appendix~\ref{app:diagnosis}).

Figure~\ref{fig:budget}b shows the curve under our objective. The panels differ in clip length as
well as objective (16 against 32 frames), so the controlled objective comparison is
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}; what the figure establishes is the direction of each curve.

\subsection{Seeds and inference cost}
\label{sec:seeds}

Two seeds of the 60k model were trained and evaluated on the complete six-dataset grid: they give
mean MSE/MAE of $0.2852/0.3279$ and $0.2838/0.3272$, a spread of $0.0014$ and $0.0007$.
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the first, which is the weaker of the two. Inference is not single-pass everywhere: the rule averages
frame scales on short-horizon cells and rolls out beyond one prediction block, so 9 of the 24 cells cost two or three
forward passes (Appendix~\ref{app:inference}), and variants that did not improve on the
reported system, including a two-seed prediction ensemble, are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:failed}.
