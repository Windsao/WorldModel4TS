\section{A transfer framework from video pretraining to forecasting}
\label{sec:method}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\input{figures/overview}

\paragraph{Overview.} The framework is one pipeline, run in two directions
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}):
\begin{center}
\small
observed series $\rightarrow$ context-only normalisation $\rightarrow$ period-aligned frames
$\rightarrow$ visible past / masked future $\rightarrow$ video backbone $\rightarrow$ predicted
frames $\rightarrow$ numeric read-out $\rightarrow$ \{forecasting loss, forecast\}.
\end{center}
During time-series continual pretraining the last step produces a loss; at test time it produces the
forecast. The design question is what has to be true of the middle of that pipeline for weights
learned on natural video to be worth anything at the end of it, and the three subsections below give
our three answers: a representation in which frames carry temporal evolution
(\S\ref{sec:render}), a training signal that is a forecast rather than a reconstruction
(\S\ref{sec:objective}), and a match between the temporal scales seen in training and those used at
inference (\S\ref{sec:scale}).

\subsection{Three stages, and what zero-shot refers to}
\label{sec:stages}

\textbf{(i) Video pretraining} is inherited: a public VideoMAE-B checkpoint trained on Kinetics-400
\citep{videomae}, and in the initialisation study V-JEPA checkpoints \citep{vjepa2}.
\textbf{(ii) Time-series continual pretraining} is the stage this paper designs, on a corpus
containing none of the evaluation datasets (\S\ref{sec:corpus}). \textbf{(iii) Zero-shot
evaluation} applies the resulting checkpoint with no parameter update and no per-dataset tuning.
Design decisions and checkpoint selection used held-out pretraining data only.

\subsection{A. Period-aligned video representation}
\label{sec:render}

A series is turned into a video, not into an image. Let $P$ be the dominant period, taken from the
sampling frequency as in \citet{visionts}, and let $k$ be the number of periods packed into one
frame. A clip of $F$ frames covers $FkP$ observations: frame $f$ holds the observations of periods
$[fk,(f+1)k)$, drawn as an area chart in a $224\times224$ frame. Within a frame the horizontal axis
is phase inside the period and the vertical axis is value; \emph{across} frames the axis is time
measured in periods. Consecutive frames therefore differ by whatever changed from one period to the
next --- level, amplitude, shape --- and a seasonal series renders as an almost static video while a
drifting or regime-switching one renders as a moving one.

This is what separates the representation from image folding. Folding a series into a 2-D image also
aligns periods, but the model then sees one static picture to complete. Here the period axis is the
model's \emph{time} axis --- the axis a video backbone was pretrained to extrapolate along --- and
the masked region is a block of future frames.

Concretely, values are standardised with the mean and standard deviation of the visible context
only, clipped to $z\in[-3,3]$, and mapped to a column height
\begin{equation}
h \;=\; 0.5 + 0.4\,\frac{z}{3}\;\in\;[0.1,\,0.9],
\label{eq:height}
\end{equation}
with the column filled below the boundary row $\mathrm{round}\big((1-h)(\mathrm{IMG}-1)\big)$ and
background above it. The drawing constants and the grid are given in Appendix~\ref{app:impl}. The
main model uses $F=32$ frames with a tubelet size of 2, giving 16 temporal positions; $F$ is a
context-length setting rather than a contribution, and we report what changes with it in
Appendix~\ref{app:16f}.

\subsection{B. Forecast-aligned prediction and supervision}
\label{sec:objective}

\paragraph{Predict a future block, not a random subset.} The trailing $h_p$ frames of the clip are
masked and must be predicted from the visible past. This is the formulation the title refers to: the model
maps an observed past to a predicted future state of the series and is scored on that prediction, in
the sense of \citet{lecun2022}, and unlike the backbone's native masked-reconstruction objective it
never sees a neighbour on the far side of what it is predicting. With probability $0.3$ the leading tubelets are
additionally hidden so that shorter contexts are also trained on, which is the regime the shorter
evaluation datasets present at test time. A fraction of batches retains the backbone's original tube
masking, as a regulariser; Appendix~\ref{app:impl} gives the exact schedule and which loss applies
to which batch type.

\paragraph{Supervise in value space.} A predicted frame is a picture, and the quantity the task is
scored on is a number, so the decoder that converts one into the other belongs inside the loss. For
a predicted frame $\hat{x}$ we compute a differentiable soft occupancy per pixel and sum it down
each column,
\begin{equation}
s(\hat{x}_{r,c}) = \mathrm{clamp}\!\left(\frac{g_{\mathrm{grid}} - \hat{x}_{r,c}}
{g_{\mathrm{grid}}-g_{\mathrm{fill}}},\,0,\,1\right),
\qquad
\hat{h}_c = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{IMG} - \sum_{r} s(\hat{x}_{r,c})}{\mathrm{IMG}-1},
\label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
which inverts the drawing rule of Equation~\ref{eq:height} up to rounding: the thresholds are chosen
so that background and grid pixels contribute zero and filled pixels contribute one, and partially
filled boundary pixels contribute fractionally. Inverting Equation~\ref{eq:height} gives
$z=(h-0.5)\cdot 3/0.4$, and the training objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{x}, x)\;+\;\lambda\left(\tfrac{3}{0.4}\right)^{2}
\mathrm{MSE}\big(\hat{h}_c,\,h_c\big),\qquad \lambda=0.1 .
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The constant converts height units into the units of the context-standardised series, so the second
term is a squared error on values rather than on gray levels. It is computed column-wise and is
therefore independent of $P$ and of how many periods a frame holds. The pixel term is retained
because early in training the read-out of a blurry prediction carries little signal.

One clarification about units: this error is in the \emph{context-instance} z-scale, since each
window is standardised by its own visible statistics, while the benchmark reports errors in a scale
fitted on the target dataset's training split. The two differ by the ratio of those standard
deviations, which varies per window. The objective is therefore an error on values rather than on
pixels, which is the property we need, but it is not numerically identical to the benchmark
metric.

\subsection{C. Temporal-scale alignment}
\label{sec:scale}

A forecaster must cover horizons from one period to many, and in this representation the horizon a
single predicted block can cover is $h_p k P$. Inference therefore varies $k$ with the requested
horizon (\S\ref{sec:inference}), and training must cover the same range or the model meets
resolutions it never saw. We sample $k\in\{1,2,4\}$ during training with probabilities
$0.5/0.3/0.2$, which is the distribution the inference rule draws from. This is a
training--inference consistency property of the framework rather than a generic augmentation: the
set of scales is determined by the inference rule, not chosen for diversity.

\subsection{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

Given the period $P$ and the requested horizon $H$, a closed-form rule selects the number of periods
per frame and the number of forward passes. The rule contains no dataset identity and is applied
unchanged to all 24 cells, but its two thresholds were chosen by comparing configurations on
development runs over four of the six datasets, so it is dataset-agnostic in form rather than
selected without reference to the benchmark; we flag this as a limitation in
Section~\ref{sec:analysis}. Short
horizons are covered by a single predicted block whose outputs are averaged over the admissible
scales; long horizons increase $k$ and, when one block still cannot span $H$, roll the prediction
forward autoregressively. The resulting forecast is truncated to $H$. Multi-scale averaging and
rollout both cost additional forward passes, so the system is single-\emph{checkpoint} and
single-\emph{rule} but not single-pass; Appendix~\ref{app:inference} gives the configuration and the
pass count for each of the 24 evaluation cells.

\subsection{Pretraining corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

The corpus is built from LOTSA \citep{moirai}: 98 subset directories, of which 74 contribute at
least one qualifying series, giving 184{,}013 series and 654.2M observations; a 5\% holdout leaves
621.9M reachable by training. Subsets are drawn in proportion to the square root of their size, and
with probability $0.2$ a window is replaced by a synthetic series generated on the fly, so the
synthetic data sits on top of the 654.2M rather than inside it. Sampling is with replacement, so the
60k-update run draws 1.92M windows of $\approx$2.9k observations each, about seven passes over the
reachable corpus, while the 5k-update run of Section~\ref{sec:backbone} sees less than one.

Two facts about the corpus deserve to be stated rather than buried. No ETT data of any kind is in it.
But it is not domain-filtered: it contains Monash \texttt{weather} (a daily Australian dataset, not
the 10-minute Jena set used as the LSF weather benchmark) and Monash \texttt{traffic\_hourly}, which
is the same freeway-occupancy source as the LSF traffic benchmark. Traffic is therefore excluded from
every table in this paper. An exact-match leakage screen over the benchmark files returned no
matches, but it was run against an earlier, domain-excluded build of the corpus rather than this one,
so we report it as a screen we have not yet repeated on the corpus actually used
(Appendix~\ref{app:impl}).
