\begin{abstract}

Visual foundation models forecast time series well: render the series as an image, hand it to an
ImageNet masked autoencoder, read the completion back as numbers. Video backbones look like the
obvious next step, since they are pretrained on how things change rather than on how they look. They
are not, at least not as shipped. Continuing VideoMAE's masked-pixel objective on rendered series
drives the held-out pixel loss down monotonically, from $0.0366$ to $0.0312$, while forecast error
climbs monotonically over the same range, from $0.339$ to $0.381$; at nine times the budget
individual benchmark cells degrade by $5$--$60\%$. Pretraining and forecasting are not merely
loosely coupled here, they point in opposite directions. We remove the mismatch by training the
backbone as a world model over rendered series: the model sees past frames, predicts future frames,
and a differentiable read-out decodes those frames back to values so that the loss is measured in
standardised value units rather than in gray levels. Combined with period-aligned rendering,
frame-scale augmentation and a 32-frame clip, the budget curve inverts and becomes monotonically
improving. The resulting zero-shot forecaster reaches a six-dataset mean MSE of $0.285$ on the
standard long-sequence benchmark, against $0.289$ for VisionTS++ large and $0.291$ for its base
model, using about $1/13$ of their continual-pretraining steps and $0.3\%$ of the data. At matched
size and matched budget, two video backbones beat an image backbone by $3$--$5\%$ at every horizon
and tie with each other, while the same architecture trained from scratch is the worst arm we ran:
what transfers from video is a prior over dynamics, not a particular pretraining objective and not
capacity.

\end{abstract}
