\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:analysis}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Two factors, separately supported.}
Two questions are easy to conflate here. Whether video pretraining is a useful starting point is
answered by Section~\ref{sec:backbone}: yes at a 5k-update budget, where both video initialisations
lead the image one and all three lead random initialisation by a wide margin. Whether a transfer
design is needed to realise it is answered by Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:diagnosis}:
yes, since the same backbone under its native objective gets worse at forecasting as that objective
improves. Our experiments support the two separately; none isolates their interaction, so we do not
attribute the end-to-end accuracy to the initialisation alone.

\paragraph{Where the initialisation advantage stops.}
The advantage does not persist. At 5k updates video initialisation leads by $3$--$5\%$ at every
horizon; at 20k the two are level (Table~\ref{tab:backbone}). Video pretraining therefore buys
adaptation efficiency rather than a higher ceiling within the budgets we can run, which is why the
headline claim is about what a limited pretraining budget reaches and not about video backbones
being better in general. The comparison also answers a question about initialisation only: the
image-initialised arm is not a stand-in for VisionTS++, which differs in rendering, head and
schedule.

\paragraph{Why the forecast-aligned objective helps more at long horizons.}
Removing the value-space term costs $5\%$ at $H{=}96$ and $8\%$ at $H{=}720$
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). A plausible reading is that short horizons are largely fixed by the
visible context, so both criteria rank completions similarly, while longer ones are generated rather
than interpolated and a completion can be smooth and pixel-cheap while sitting at the wrong level.
The ablation is consistent with this and does not establish it.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Mid-to-long horizons on the hourly ETT sets remain behind the strongest baselines: the context is
capped at 32 frames of at most four periods, so covering $H{=}720$ with $P{=}24$ trades resolution
for coverage. Mean MAE is $0.328$ against $0.326$, which is worse, not equal, and we have no
uncertainty estimate that would justify calling it a tie. The evaluation covers one six-dataset protocol; GIFT-Eval
\citep{gifteval} and Monash \citep{monash} are not run. Seed replication exists for the 20k model only, so the headline number carries no error bar; budget
is reported in corpus size, updates and sample presentations, not FLOPs or wall-clock, and excludes
the inherited video pretraining; and the ViT-B backbone comparison is size-matched rather than
architecturally exact. Finally, the corpus contains Monash \texttt{traffic\_hourly}, the same data
as the LSF traffic benchmark, so traffic is excluded from our evaluation.
