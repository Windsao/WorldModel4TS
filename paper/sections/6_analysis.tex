\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:analysis}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{What the evidence supports, and what bounds it.}
Two claims stand on separate experiments. A video-pretrained backbone is a better starting point than
an image-pretrained one when the adaptation budget is small: at 5k updates, everything but the weights
held fixed, it leads by $3$--$5\%$ at every horizon, and random initialisation of the same
architecture is far behind both. And a transfer framework is what makes any of these initialisations
usable: under its own objective the same backbone gets worse at forecasting as that objective
improves, and the value-space read-out is worth $5$--$8\%$ over pixel-only supervision under a
matched ablation. Together they give the system result of Section~\ref{sec:main}; no experiment
separates their interaction, so we attribute the end-to-end accuracy to the framework as a whole.

The video advantage closes by 20k updates (Table~\ref{tab:backbone}). We keep that beside the claim
it bounds, because it says what the video prior supplies: not a higher attainable accuracy but a
lower cost of reaching it --- the quantity a small adaptation budget is short of, and what makes the
headline result a statement about efficiency. At a large adaptation budget, the expectation it sets
is that the visual prior matters less than the transfer design.

\paragraph{Limitations that bound the central claim.}
Our lead is on MSE only: against the same-size VisionTS++ base we take 17 of 24 cells on MSE but 11
on MAE, and the mean MAE is $0.328$ against $0.326$. The video initialisation's advantage is a
5k-update result and is gone by 20k. The budget comparison is in corpus size, optimizer updates and
sample presentations, not FLOPs, and excludes the upstream video pretraining we inherit. Appendix~\ref{app:scope} collects the
caveats that scope the work rather than the claim.
