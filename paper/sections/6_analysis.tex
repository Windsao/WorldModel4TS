\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:analysis}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Two factors, separately supported.}
Two questions are easy to conflate here. Whether video pretraining is a useful starting point is
answered by Section~\ref{sec:backbone}: yes at a 5k-update budget, where both video initialisations
lead the image one and all three lead random initialisation by a wide margin. Whether a transfer
design is needed to realise it is answered by Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:diagnosis}:
yes, since the same backbone under its native objective gets worse at forecasting as that objective
improves. Our experiments support the two separately; none isolates their interaction, so we do not attribute
the end-to-end accuracy to the initialisation alone. The value-space term's benefit also grows with
horizon ($5\%$ at $H{=}96$, $8\%$ at $H{=}720$), consistent with long horizons being generated
rather than interpolated, though the ablation does not establish that mechanism.

\paragraph{Where the initialisation advantage stops.}
The advantage does not persist. At 5k updates video initialisation leads by $3$--$5\%$ at every
horizon; at 20k the two are level (Table~\ref{tab:backbone}). Video pretraining therefore buys
adaptation efficiency rather than a higher ceiling within the budgets we can run, which is why the
headline claim is about what a limited pretraining budget reaches and not about video backbones
being better in general. The comparison also answers a question about initialisation only: the
image-initialised arm is not a stand-in for VisionTS++, which differs in rendering, head and
schedule.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our advantage is metric-dependent: against the same-size VisionTS++ base we win 17 of 24 cells on
MSE but only 11 on MAE, and the mean MAE is $0.328$ against $0.326$, which is worse, not equal.
Mid-to-long horizons on the hourly ETT sets remain behind, because the context is capped at 32
frames of at most four periods. The second seed of the 60k model was still missing its electricity
cells at the time of writing, so the headline number is a single seed. The inference rule carries no
dataset identity, but its two thresholds were chosen on development runs over four of the six
datasets, so it was not selected blind to the benchmark. The exact-match leakage screen that returned
no matches was run against an earlier, domain-excluded build of the corpus rather than the one these
models used; that corpus contains Monash \texttt{weather} (a different source from the LSF weather
benchmark) and \texttt{traffic\_hourly} (the same source as the LSF traffic benchmark, which is why
traffic appears nowhere here). Finally, the evaluation is one six-dataset protocol, budget is
reported in corpus size, updates and sample presentations rather than FLOPs, and the ViT-B backbone
comparison is size-matched rather than architecturally exact.
