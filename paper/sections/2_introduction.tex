\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Zero-shot forecasters are trained once and applied to datasets they have never seen. The models that
do this well today --- Moirai \citep{moirai}, Chronos \citep{chronos}, Time-MoE \citep{timemoe},
TimesFM \citep{timesfm} --- get there by pretraining on time-series corpora measured in tens or
hundreds of billions of observations. That is a reasonable way to spend resources if one has them,
and a barrier if one does not. The question we take up is how far a competitive zero-shot forecaster
can be pushed when the time-series pretraining budget is small, by starting from a model that has
already learned something about how signals evolve.

One answer already exists in the visual direction. VisionTS \citep{visionts} renders a series as an
image and lets a frozen ImageNet masked autoencoder \citep{mae} inpaint the missing right-hand
portion; VisionTS++ \citep{visiontspp} continues that backbone's pretraining on rendered series and
reports the strongest published zero-shot numbers on the standard long-sequence protocol. Both
inherit a prior over spatial structure from a model that has never seen anything change over time.
Video pretraining offers a different inheritance: a representation built from sequences, and, in
the predictive variants, an explicit forward model \citep{videomae, vjepa2}. Whether that buys
anything for forecasting is an empirical question, and the useful form of it is about efficiency
rather than ceiling: \emph{does a video-pretrained backbone reach a given forecasting accuracy under
a smaller time-series adaptation budget?}

Answering it is not a matter of swapping the backbone. A numeric series has to become a video whose
axes mean something; masked reconstruction recovers a token from neighbours on both sides rather
than predicting a future from a past; and the error the model minimises is measured in gray levels
while the task is scored in the units of the series. These gaps are not cosmetic: continuing
VideoMAE's native objective on a 654M-observation corpus of rendered series drives its held-out
pixel loss down monotonically and its forecast error up monotonically across the checkpoints we
evaluated (Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis}).

We therefore treat the video backbone as an initialisation for a forecasting model and build the
training stage around the forecasting task. Three design choices carry the framework
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). \textbf{Period-aligned rendering} lays one period across the horizontal
axis of a frame and value on the vertical axis, so that consecutive frames express evolution from
one period to the next rather than continuation of a line. \textbf{Forecast-aligned prediction and
supervision} masks a trailing block of frames, predicts it from the visible past only, and decodes
the predicted frames back into values with a differentiable read-out, so the training loss is
computed on numbers rather than on pixels. \textbf{Temporal-scale alignment} samples during training
the same periods-per-frame scales the inference rule will use, so the model is trained on the
resolutions it is asked to work at. Together they make the inherited weights usable: the budget
curve that ran backwards under the native objective runs forwards under this one.

Under this framework, 60k optimizer updates at an effective batch of 32 on 654M observations --- a
corpus roughly $0.3\%$ the size of the LOTSA archive VisionTS++ trains on, and roughly $1/27$ of its
sample presentations (Section~\ref{sec:budget}) --- yield one checkpoint that, under one inference
rule, reaches a mean MSE of $0.285$ over the six standard long-sequence datasets and four horizons.
That is the lowest mean MSE among the models we compare on this protocol, ahead of VisionTS++ large
($0.289$) and base ($0.291$); mean MAE is $0.328$ against their $0.326$, which is worse and we
report it as such. Changing only the initialisation shows where the video prior contributes: at a
5k-step budget both video initialisations beat an ImageNet initialisation by $3$--$5\%$ at every
horizon and random initialisation is the worst arm by a wide margin, while by 20k steps the image
initialisation has caught up --- which bounds the claim to the low-budget regime
(Section~\ref{sec:backbone}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A transfer framework that turns a video-pretrained backbone into a zero-shot time-series
      forecaster under a small time-series pretraining budget: period-aligned video rendering,
      future-block prediction supervised through a differentiable numeric read-out, and a training
      scale distribution matched to the inference rule (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item Zero-shot accuracy competitive with the strongest published visual and time-series foundation
      models on the six-dataset long-sequence protocol, from 60k updates on 654M observations, with
      the budget reported in corpus size, optimizer updates and sample presentations rather than as
      a compute claim (Sections~\ref{sec:main} and~\ref{sec:budget}).
\item Controlled evidence for where the gain comes from and where it stops: an initialisation study
      at matched size and budget with a random-init control, an ablation of the forecast-aligned
      objective, and the diagnosis of why naive continual pretraining fails
      (Sections~\ref{sec:backbone}--\ref{sec:diagnosis}).
\end{itemize}
