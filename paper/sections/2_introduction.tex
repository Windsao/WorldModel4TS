\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A time series can be drawn. Once drawn, an image model can finish the drawing, and the finished
drawing can be read back as numbers. VisionTS \citep{visionts} showed that this detour beats most
purpose-built forecasters in the zero-shot setting, and VisionTS++ \citep{visiontspp} showed that
continuing the image model's pretraining on a large corpus of rendered series makes it the strongest
zero-shot forecaster published. Both use an ImageNet masked autoencoder \citep{mae}. Neither has
ever seen anything move.

Video backbones are the natural next candidate. They are pretrained on how signals evolve, which is
what forecasting asks about, and the recent ones are explicitly built as world models: V-JEPA~2
\citep{vjepa2} predicts the latent state of the future from the past and is used for planning. If a
prior over dynamics is worth anything for forecasting, a video backbone should be where it lives.

We started from that premise and it failed, in a way that turned out to be the interesting part of
the story. Taking VideoMAE \citep{videomae} and continuing its native masked-pixel objective on a
654M-observation corpus of rendered series does improve the pretraining objective --- held-out pixel
loss falls from $0.0366$ at 5k steps to $0.0312$ at 176k --- while downstream forecast error rises
over exactly the same range, from $0.339$ to $0.381$ (Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis}). This is not
noise and not overfitting: the training loss stays flat, and the degradation is largest at the
longest horizons, where one benchmark cell moves from $0.429$ to $0.617$. More pretraining on more
data, with the objective the backbone was designed around, makes the forecaster worse every time we
spend more compute on it.

The reason is that the native objective never asks the question we care about. Masked pixel
reconstruction is bidirectional and interpolative: a token is recovered from neighbours on both
sides, and the model is free to spend capacity on grid lines, anti-aliasing and fill boundaries,
none of which carry value information. The mapping from pixels back to numbers --- the step that
actually determines forecast error --- is applied only at test time and is never trained against.
Two models can differ by $8\%$ in gray-level error and by $12\%$ in the opposite direction in
forecast error, because the losses measure different things.

Our fix is to make the pretraining task the forecasting task. We train the backbone as a world model
over rendered series: the last frames of a clip are masked, the model predicts them from the visible
past, and a differentiable soft row-count decodes the predicted frames back into values so that the
training loss includes an error term in standardised value units. Nothing about the backbone
changes, only what it is asked to be right about. Around this we place three choices that matter
at the level of a percent or two each: rendering one period per frame so that vertical position
encodes value and horizontal position encodes phase, sampling the periods-per-frame scale during
training to match the multi-scale inference rule, and using 32-frame clips instead of 16.

The budget curve inverts. Under the aligned objective, five-dataset mean MSE improves monotonically
with training --- $0.250$ at 5k steps, $0.245$ at 20k, $0.242$ at 60k --- where the pixel objective
degraded monotonically over the same range. The final model reaches a six-dataset mean MSE of
$0.285$ on the standard long-sequence forecasting protocol, ahead of VisionTS++ large ($0.289$) and
base ($0.291$), Moirai base ($0.311$) and VisionTS ($0.315$), at roughly $1/13$ of VisionTS++'s
continual-pretraining steps and $0.3\%$ of its data. Against the only baseline that publishes
per-horizon numbers on this protocol, we win 18 of 24 cells.

A matched-size study then asks what the video backbone was contributing in the first place. Holding
the recipe, corpus, budget and inference rule fixed and swapping only the initialisation, an
ImageNet MAE-B reaches $0.264$ at $H{=}96$, VideoMAE-B $0.250$, V-JEPA~2.1-B $0.252$, and the same
V-JEPA architecture initialised randomly $0.280$ --- worse than an image backbone $3.5\times$
smaller. The two video objectives, one reconstructive and one predictive, are indistinguishable from
each other while both beat the image backbone. What transfers is a prior over dynamics; the
particular objective that produced it is not what matters, and capacity does not explain it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A diagnosis: on rendered time series, masked-pixel continual pretraining and forecasting
      accuracy move in opposite directions across a $35\times$ range of training budget
      (Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis}).
\item A forecast-aligned world-model objective that removes the mismatch by decoding predicted
      frames back to values with a differentiable read-out and training on the value-space error
      (Section~\ref{sec:method}). It accounts for the entire gain in our ablation, and the gap grows
      with horizon.
\item State of the art on the standard zero-shot long-sequence benchmark at a fraction of the
      pretraining budget: $0.285$ mean MSE with one checkpoint and one inference rule for all 24
      cells (Section~\ref{sec:main}).
\item Evidence that the transferable ingredient is a dynamics prior rather than a specific video
      objective or model capacity, from a matched-size backbone comparison with a random-init
      control (Section~\ref{sec:backbone}).
\end{itemize}
